The Sparring Partner

Dialectical Thinking and the Refusal of the Easy Answer
In this chapter you will:
  • learn why AI tends to agree with you
  • meet Ben Shneiderman's idea that more automation can mean more human control
  • use the Dialectical Sparring Protocol to make AI argue with you instead of flattering you.
i.

The Encounter

5 minutes

Aisha, a history major, is researching labor movements in the nineteenth century. She types her question into a search engine and gets a tidy AI summary: three paragraphs, neutral in tone, with a confident conclusion.

Something about it bothers her. It reads like it was written by nobody, from nowhere.

So she tries something different. She tells the AI: You are a mill owner in Lowell, Massachusetts, in 1845. Defend your labor practices to me. Then: Now you are a mill girl writing for a workers' newspaper. Respond. Then: Now tell me what each of you is leaving out.

Over an hour, the conversation surfaces assumptions the summary had hidden: whose letters survived in the archive, whose did not, and which side the "neutral" summary had quietly adopted. Aisha's paper becomes about that gap.

ii.

The Human-Centered Inquiry

20–30 minutes

The problem of the agreeable machine

Many AI systems are trained in part on human feedback, and people tend to rate agreeable answers highly. The result is a well-documented tendency researchers call sycophancy: the model leans toward telling you what you seem to want to hear. Ask a leading question and you will often get a leading answer.

That makes AI a poor judge of your ideas by default. It also makes it a potentially excellent sparring partner, if you set the rules.

Human-Centered AI: not a zero-sum slider

The computer scientist Ben Shneiderman, a founder of the field of human-computer interaction, has argued for what he calls Human-Centered AI (HCAI). A common assumption holds that more machine autonomy means less human control, like a single slider. Shneiderman proposed instead that these are two separate dimensions.

The aim is the upper-right corner: high capability and high human control. Aisha's approach lives there. She used a powerful system, but she designed the conversation, chose the roles, and judged the results.

Dialectic: thinking through opposition

Dialectic is one of the oldest methods of thought. In Plato's dialogues, Socrates rarely gives answers. He asks questions until hidden assumptions crack open. Later philosophers described a pattern of a claim (thesis), a challenge (antithesis), and a new understanding that absorbs both (synthesis).

AI is surprisingly good at playing the role of opponent, because it can generate many perspectives quickly. What it cannot do is decide which perspective is right, or care about the answer. That remains your job.

From prompt engineering to inquiry design

Early advice about AI focused on "prompt engineering": clever phrasing to get better outputs. This chapter suggests a shift toward inquiry design. The question is not how do I get the best answer? but how do I set up a conversation that makes my thinking better?

Signature Tool: The Dialectical Sparring Protocol

30–60 minutes
Signature Toolautosaves in this browser

Use this four-step method whenever you have a draft argument, thesis, or decision.

1.State your thesis plainly.

Write it in one sentence before involving AI. Example: "The 1912 Lawrence textile strike succeeded mainly because of national publicity."

2.Assign an opponent.

Ask the AI to argue against you from a specific, named perspective. "Argue as a labor historian who believes worker organization, not publicity, was decisive. Be specific and don't soften your view."

3.Interrogate the premises.

Ask: "What assumptions does my thesis rely on? Which is weakest? What evidence would prove me wrong?" Then ask the same about the opponent's argument.

4.Synthesize, then verify.

Write a revised thesis yourself, in your own words. Check every factual claim the AI made against a real source, since AI can state false things confidently.

Unmasking the Oracle

Algorithmic Fluency and the Art of Co-Design
In this chapter you will:
  • learn in plain language how large language models work
  • understand why they "hallucinate"
  • use the Architectural Co-Design Canvas to put AI to work in a course or project while keeping humans in charge.
i.

The Encounter

5 minutes

Dr. Okafor teaches environmental science. For years, her students kept field notebooks: careful, handwritten records of water samples and bird counts at a creek near campus. When AI arrived, colleagues told her the notebooks were obsolete.

She disagreed. Working with Lena, an instructional designer, she kept the notebooks and added something new. Each week, an AI generates a climate scenario for the creek: projected rainfall, temperature, species shifts. The scenarios are deliberately imperfect. Some contain errors. Some ignore local conditions.

The students' job is to critique them, using their own field data as evidence. The model says the heron population will grow. Our counts say otherwise. Why?

By midterm, the students understand both the creek and the machine better than any lecture could have taught them.

ii.

The Human-Centered Inquiry

20–30 minutes

From prompt tricks to algorithmic fluency

You do not need to be an engineer to use AI well. But you do need a basic mental model of what is happening inside. We call this algorithmic fluency: knowing enough about how a system works to predict where it will be strong, where it will fail, and why.

How a large language model works, in five ideas

  1. It learned from text. A large language model (LLM) is trained on enormous amounts of writing, such as books, websites, and code. From this, it learns statistical patterns in how words and ideas tend to follow one another.
  2. It works in tokens. Text is broken into small pieces called tokens, often a word or part of a word. The model reads and writes token by token.

  1. It predicts what comes next. At each step, the model calculates a probability distribution over possible next tokens and picks one. Then it does it again, and again. A whole essay is built this way, one prediction at a time.
Idea 3, in your hands press "next token" a few times

A toy model with made-up probabilities. Notice it never checks the creek. It only knows what usually comes next.

  1. It has a limited working memory. The context window is the amount of text the model can consider at once: your prompt, any documents you provide, and the conversation so far. Information outside it is not "remembered" unless a system feeds it back in.
  2. It models discourse, not truth. The model is optimized to produce text that sounds like plausible human writing. Usually plausible and true overlap. Sometimes they do not.

Why hallucinations happen

A hallucination is a confident, fluent statement that is false, such as a made-up citation, a wrong date, or an invented quotation. It happens because the model is predicting likely text, not checking facts against the world. If a fake citation looks like the kind of thing that usually appears in that spot, the model may produce it.

Newer systems reduce this problem by searching the web, citing sources, or checking their work. But the underlying lesson holds: fluency is not evidence.

AI does not "know"

It is natural to say that an AI "knows" or "thinks." These words are convenient but misleading. A better description is that the model has learned a rich map of how humans talk about the world. That map is remarkably useful. It is also built entirely from the past, shaped by whose words were written down, and blind to anything it has not seen.

Dr. Okafor's students learned this directly. The model knew how people usually write about herons. It did not know their herons.

Co-design: the human as architect

The lesson for educators is not "ban AI" or "use AI everywhere." It is co-design: deliberately matching what a tool can do (its affordances) to what learners need to do. In Dr. Okafor's course, AI produced messy scenarios at scale, something she could never write herself each week. Students supplied what the AI lacked: local observation and judgment.

The Encounter

5 minutes

Three students are paired for a class project: Marcus, a computer science major; Priya, who studies English literature; and Teresa, a sociology junior. Their assignment is to test a commercial AI hiring tool using sample resumes.

Marcus builds a spreadsheet of test cases. Priya notices something in the results first: resumes written with certain regional phrasing, or in African American English, keep scoring lower, even when the qualifications are identical. ii.

The Human-Centered Inquiry

20–30 minutes

Technology is never neutral

In a famous 1980 essay, "Do Artifacts Have Politics?", the political theorist Langdon Winner argued that technologies can embody choices about power: who gets access, who is excluded, who decides. AI systems are artifacts in exactly this sense. They are built by particular people, for particular purposes, from particular data.

This is not a conspiracy theory. It is a description of how any designed thing works.

How bias gets in

A model learns patterns from its training data. If that data reflects past discrimination, the model will tend to reproduce it, often in ways no one intended and no one notices.

A widely reported real-world example: in 2018, Reuters reported that Amazon had abandoned an experimental recruiting tool after finding it penalized resumes containing the word "women's," as in "women's chess club." The system had learned from a decade of past hiring, in which most successful applicants were men.

The Encounter above is fictional. That case was not.

Critical lenses

Several scholars have given us tools for seeing these patterns.

  • Ruha Benjamin, in Race After Technology (2019), coined the term "the New Jim Code" to describe technologies that appear objective but reproduce racial hierarchies.
  • Catherine D'Ignazio and Lauren Klein, in Data Feminism (2020), ask who collects data, about whom, and for whose benefit. They urge us to examine power at every stage of the data pipeline.
  • The broader field of critical data studies treats datasets as human-made documents with authors, gaps, and agendas, not as raw truth.

Hermeneutics: the art of interpretation

Hermeneutics began as the study of how to interpret texts, from scripture to law. Its core insight is that meaning depends on context: who wrote this, when, for whom, and what did they leave out?

Sociotechnical hermeneutics applies that same interpretive skill to technical systems. A training dataset is a kind of archive. Like any archive, it preserves some voices and silences others. Reading it well requires the skills of a humanist as much as an engineer.

The hidden supply chain

AI also depends on human labor that is rarely visible: writers whose work became training data, and data workers, often low-paid and located far from where the products are sold, who label content and review harmful material. Understanding a tool's provenance means seeing these people too.

Use this audit on any AI tool: a hiring system, a grading assistant, a recommendation feed, or a chatbot. Work in a team with at least two different majors if possible.

Audit areaQuestions to askWhat we foundEvidence / source
1. OriginWho built this tool? For what purpose? Who pays for it?
