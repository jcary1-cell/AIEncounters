The Relational Commons

Voice, Presence, and Community

A commons is a shared space that belongs to everyone: a village green, a public library, a classroom. Act III argues that the most valuable things AI cannot produce live in this space: a voice that is recognizably yours, and trust between people who are present to one another.

The Sovereign Voice

Co-Writing, Digital Storytelling, and Rhetorical Integrity
In this chapter you will:
  • understand why authentic voice is becoming more valuable, not less
  • explore how we present ourselves in digital spaces
  • use the Sovereign Voice Index to co-write with AI without disappearing from your own writing.
i.

The Encounter

5 minutes

Rosa is a first-generation college senior applying for jobs in public health. She asks an AI to write her cover letters. They are flawless: confident, well-organized, full of phrases like "passionate about driving impact" and "proven track record of cross-functional collaboration."

She reads one aloud to her roommate. Her roommate laughs. That's not you.

Rosa realizes the letters leave out everything that makes her a strong candidate: translating medical forms for her grandmother at age eleven, the summer she spent organizing a vaccination drive at her church, the reason she cares about public health at all. The AI did not know those stories. Worse, Rosa had started to think they were not "professional" enough to include.

She rewrites the letter herself, keeping a few of the AI's structural ideas. It is less polished. It gets three callbacks.

ii.

The Human-Centered Inquiry

20–30 minutes

The flood of sameness

Generative AI makes competent prose almost free. When everyone can produce a polished cover letter, polish stops being a signal. Readers such as hiring managers, admissions officers, and professors begin to recognize the generic phrasing and the familiar rhythm, and to skim past it.

In a world of abundant synthetic text, the scarce thing is voice: writing that could only have come from one particular person, with their experiences, values, and way of seeing.

Voice is more than style

Voice includes:

The five parts of voice which can a machine imitate?
  • Cadence: the rhythm of your sentences
  • Diction: the words you naturally reach for
  • Stance: what you believe and how firmly you say it
  • Narrative authority: the right to tell a story because you lived it
  • Moral clarity: a visible sense of what matters to you

AI can imitate cadence and diction. It cannot supply stance, lived narrative, or moral clarity. Those must come from you.

Presenting the self

The sociologist Erving Goffman, in The Presentation of Self in Everyday Life (1959), described social life as a kind of performance. We manage how others see us, adjusting our "front" for different audiences.

Digital life adds new layers to this performance. Profiles, posts, and applications are all curated selves. AI makes curation easier still, which raises a question Goffman could not have anticipated: If a machine wrote my front, is it still my performance?

The ethics of co-writing

Co-writing with AI is not inherently dishonest. Writers have always used editors, templates, and feedback. The ethical questions are about transparency (would your reader feel misled if they knew?) and integrity (does the final piece truly represent what you think and who you are?).

A useful rule: AI can help you say it better. It should not decide what you say.

The Architecture of the Handshake

Relational Pedagogy and the Non-Transactional Classroom
In this chapter you will:
  • see why AI-detection surveillance often backfires
  • rediscover education as a relationship built on trust
  • use the Syllabus as Handshake Framework to write course policies with students instead of at them.
i.

The Encounter

5 minutes

The English department at a mid-sized university adopts AI-detection software. Within one semester, the problems pile up. A student who learned English as a second language is flagged three times for essays she wrote alone. A senior with a careful, formal style is accused of cheating on his thesis chapter. Office hours fill with students arguing about percentages on a screen.

Faculty spend more time investigating than teaching. Students start running their own work through detectors before submitting, rewriting sentences to look "more human."

At a tense department meeting, a senior professor says: We have built a system where everyone is a suspect. I don't recognize my own classroom.

The department drops the software. In its place, each major assignment ends with a short project defense: a ten-minute conversation in which the student explains their choices, their sources, and what they would change. Cheating does not vanish. But trust returns, and so does teaching.

ii.

The Human-Centered Inquiry

20–30 minutes

The trouble with detection

AI-detection tools estimate the likelihood that text was machine-generated. Independent research has found they can be unreliable, and a 2023 Stanford study found that several detectors frequently misclassified essays by non-native English speakers as AI-written. When a tool's errors fall hardest on particular groups of students, its use becomes a fairness problem, not just a technical one.

Even a perfect detector would leave a deeper problem untouched. It treats the classroom as a site of enforcement rather than learning.

Machines talking to machines

Imagine the end point of current trends. A student submits an AI-written essay. An instructor uses AI to generate feedback. The student uses AI to revise in response. Each side is efficient. No one has actually met.

Machines talking to machines put the people back

This is education reduced to machines talking to machines across an empty classroom. It satisfies every requirement on paper and fails the only one that matters: a human being changed by an encounter with another human being and with ideas.

Education as relationship

The educator and writer bell hooks, in Teaching to Transgress (1994), described engaged pedagogy: teaching that treats students as whole people and the classroom as a community where everyone's presence matters. Nel Noddings, whom we met in Chapter 3, grounded education in care and attention.

Both point to the same truth. Learning involves vulnerability: being willing to be wrong in front of someone. Vulnerability requires trust. Surveillance destroys it.

The syllabus as a handshake

A syllabus is usually written as a contract, full of rules, penalties, and fine print. But it can also be a handshake: a mutual agreement in which both sides commit to something. The instructor promises clarity, fairness, and genuine feedback. Students promise honest effort and transparency about how they work, including how they use AI.

Feedback as mentorship

If students should not outsource their thinking, instructors should be cautious about outsourcing their feedback. Feedback is one of the most personal acts in teaching. It says I read your work, I thought about you, and here is what I see. AI can help instructors organize or speed up feedback, but the judgment and the care should remain visibly human.

Schedule a mid-semester check-in to revise the handshake together.
