ii.

The Human-Centered Inquiry

20–30 minutes

The atomized job seeker

Many employers now screen applications with automated systems that filter resumes by keywords and criteria before any human reads them. Applicants respond by using AI to write more applications, faster. Employers respond with more automation. The result is an arms race in which each side becomes more invisible to the other.

The hidden social cost is atomization: job seekers are isolated, each alone with a screen, competing with thousands of strangers through a process none of them can see.

The strength of weak ties

In 1973, the sociologist Mark Granovetter published one of the most cited papers in social science, "The Strength of Weak Ties." His finding surprised people. Job seekers often got useful leads not from close friends (strong ties) but from acquaintances (weak ties).

The reason is simple. Your close friends tend to know the same people and hear the same news you do. Acquaintances move in different circles, so they carry new information. A large 2022 study of LinkedIn users, published in Science, found evidence consistent with this idea: moderately weak ties were the most helpful for job mobility.

Brokers and structural holes

The sociologist Ronald Burt added another idea: structural holes. These are gaps between groups that do not talk to each other. People who bridge those gaps, called brokers, gain access to ideas and opportunities that others miss.

Eli's aunt's neighbor was a broker. She connected the world of philosophy graduates to the world of user research, two groups that rarely meet.

Translating the liberal arts

Liberal arts graduates often struggle to explain the value of their skills, especially to automated filters looking for specific keywords. But many of those skills are increasingly important in an AI economy.

An automated filter may miss these translations. A human being, in conversation, usually will not.

The informational interview

An informational interview is a short conversation in which you ask someone about their work, not for a job. It is low-pressure for both sides and often turns into referrals, advice, and introductions. It is the most human tool in the job search, and among the most effective.

The Encounter

5 minutes

Nadia is a project manager at a fast-growing logistics company. Her team uses a new AI productivity platform that tracks keystrokes, idle time, and "focus scores." One morning she reviews the monthly dashboard and notices that two of her best employees are flagged as low performers.

She digs into the data. One employee has a medical condition that requires frequent breaks. The other spends long stretches thinking and sketching on paper before typing, which the system records as "idle." The software is penalizing a disability and a working style.

Nadia could stay quiet. Instead, she gathers colleagues from HR, legal, and engineering, and proposes a cross-departmental ethical review of the tool. It is uncomfortable. It is also the most professional thing she has done all year.

ii.

The Human-Centered Inquiry

20–30 minutes

Algorithmic management

Algorithmic management means using software to assign, monitor, evaluate, and sometimes discipline workers. It began with ride-hailing and delivery apps and has spread into warehouses, call centers, and offices.

These systems promise objectivity. In practice, they measure what is easy to count, such as keystrokes, clicks, and time on task, and can miss what matters most: judgment, creativity, care, and collaboration. Echoing Chapter 1, they can also shift power. When the system defines "good work," workers lose a say in what their work is.

The scholar Shoshana Zuboff, in The Age of Surveillance Capitalism (2019), described how data extracted from human behavior became a source of profit and control. Workplace monitoring is one place that logic has landed.

Rethinking professionalism

Professionalism is often taught as reliability, politeness, and following the rules. Those are good habits. But in an automated workplace, quiet compliance can mean going along with systems that harm people.

This book proposes a fuller definition: professionalism as sociotechnical stewardship. A steward takes responsibility for something on behalf of others. A professional steward notices when a tool causes harm, raises concerns through appropriate channels, and helps fix the problem.

This is not the same as defiance for its own sake. Nadia did not sabotage the software. She gathered evidence, built allies, and proposed a better process.

Knowing your value

AI is also changing how compensation works. When parts of a role are automated, employers may argue that the job is worth less. Workers need to be able to show the value of what they uniquely contribute. That requires two things: evidence about the market, and stories about your impact.

Civic technology and the public good

Not every AI decision happens in a corporation. Governments use algorithms to decide who gets benefits, which neighborhoods get inspections, and how resources are allocated. Nonprofits use AI to reach people in need. Human-centered principles apply here too, often with higher stakes, because citizens cannot simply choose a different provider.
