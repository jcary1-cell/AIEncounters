Act V

The Institutional Frontier

Leading, Convening, and Scaling

Individuals can change how they use AI. Institutions decide the conditions under which everyone else does. Act V is written for anyone who leads, from a department chair to a provost to a student government president, and for anyone who wants to understand how leaders should act.

Chapter 11 argues that, in a period of fast and uncertain change, trust outperforms mandates. Chapter 12 makes the case for the oldest leadership tool there is: bringing people into the same room.

By the end of Act V, you will have two blueprints for building human-centered AI culture at the scale of an entire institution.

Leading Through the Fog

Shared Governance, Risk, and Ecosystem Leadership
In this chapter you will:
  • see why top-down bans on AI tend to fail
  • learn how shared governance builds durable institutional decisions
  • use the Agile Task Force Canvas to launch a cross-campus AI council.
i.

The Encounter

5 minutes

The spring that generative AI arrives on campus, the provost's inbox fills overnight. Faculty demand a ban. Students demand access. The IT office warns about data privacy. The general counsel asks about liability. A board member forwards a news article with the subject line Are we behind?

The easiest move is a campus-wide email: Effective immediately, the use of generative AI tools in coursework is prohibited. A draft sits in the provost's outbox.

She does not send it. Instead, she calls together a task force: faculty from every college, staff from IT and the library, student government representatives, legal counsel, and the teaching center. Their charge is not to write rules overnight. It is to listen, experiment, and recommend principles that can evolve.

Six months later, the campus has guidelines that faculty actually use, because faculty helped write them. The ban would have been obsolete within a semester.

ii.

The Human-Centered Inquiry

20–30 minutes

Why mandates fail in the fog

In stable conditions, clear rules work well. But AI is changing faster than policy cycles. A mandate written in March may be out of date by August, when new tools appear that it never imagined.

Top-down bans also carry hidden costs:

  • They are hard to enforce, which pushes use underground and invites the surveillance problems of Chapter 8.
  • They ignore disciplinary difference. What makes sense in a first-year writing course may make no sense in a data science lab.
  • They erode trust, signaling that leaders see faculty and students as risks to manage rather than partners to involve.

Leading through trust

Leadership scholars distinguish between technical problems, which have known solutions experts can apply, and adaptive challenges, which require people to learn, change values or habits, and invent new approaches. The leadership scholar Ronald Heifetz developed this distinction. AI in education is an adaptive challenge. No one has the answer yet.

Adaptive challenges call for a different kind of leadership: setting direction, protecting space for experimentation, and helping people learn together, rather than handing down solutions.

Shared governance as a strength

Universities have a long tradition of shared governance: faculty, administrators, and often staff and students share responsibility for major decisions. It is sometimes criticized as slow. In a period of uncertainty, it can be an advantage, because decisions made together are understood more widely, adopted more readily, and corrected faster.

Connecting the silos

AI touches every part of an institution at once. Consider who has a stake.

Unit · Its central AI concern bring each unit to the table

0 of 7 at the table. Every office is working alone.

No single office can address all of these. An effective AI council is, above all, a bridge between silos: the institutional version of Chapter 9's broker.

Block 3:Listening.

How will the council hear from people who are not in the room? Examples: listening sessions, surveys, open forums, an anonymous comment form.

Block 4:Experiments.

What small pilots will the council run or support before setting policy? Who reports back, and when?

Block 5:Principles before rules.

Draft three to five guiding principles first. Detailed rules can follow and change. Example principle: "Instructors decide how AI is used in their courses, and they explain their reasons to students."

Block 6:Communication rhythm.

How often will the council share updates, and through which channels?

Block 7:Review date.

When will the council revisit its own principles? Put it on the calendar now.

The Gathering Ground

Fostering Innovation and the UNC Charlotte Model
In this chapter you will:
  • understand why physical gathering is a powerful leadership tool in an automated age
  • see how one university built an ecosystem for AI in teaching
  • use the Accelerator Sandbox Template to design faculty programs and campus summits of your own.
i.

The Encounter

5 minutes

A philosophy professor, a mechanical engineering professor, and a computer scientist sit at the same round table at a campus AI summit. They have worked at the same university for a combined fifty years. They have never had a conversation.

The session prompt asks each table to share one assignment AI has "broken." The philosopher describes a take-home essay. The engineer describes a design report. The computer scientist describes an intro coding lab. Within ten minutes, they realize they are facing the same problem: students can now produce the artifact without developing the judgment the artifact was meant to show.

By the end of lunch, they have sketched a shared approach, the oral defense of design choices, that each will adapt in their own course. Over the next year, they keep meeting. A twenty-year-old wall between their departments develops a door.

ii.

The Human-Centered Inquiry

20–30 minutes

Why the room still matters

In an age of video calls and AI-generated summaries, gathering people in the same physical space can seem old-fashioned. It is not. Shared physical presence produces things that are hard to get any other way:

  • Serendipity: unplanned conversations between people who would never have scheduled a meeting
  • Trust: reading faces, sharing meals, and seeing colleagues as whole people
  • Momentum: the energy of seeing that many others care about the same problem
  • Permission: a visible signal from the institution that experimentation is valued

The more work is mediated by machines, the more valuable face-to-face gathering becomes. It is the institutional version of Chapter 8's handshake.

The UNC Charlotte ecosystem

UNC Charlotte offers one example of how an institution can build a human-centered AI culture through layered, connected initiatives. Each piece serves a different purpose, and together they form an ecosystem rather than a single program.

Charlotte AI Summit for Smarter Learning ↗

An annual gathering, hosted by the Center for Teaching and Learning (CTL), where faculty, staff, students, and partners share AI teaching practices. The fourth annual summit took place in May 2026.

AI Faculty Fellows

Faculty appointed by the CTL to lead events, support colleagues, and champion AI across the curriculum.

AI Faculty Use Case Stories Collection ↗

A published collection of 80 first-person faculty stories about teaching with AI, from initial skepticism to classroom redesign.

AI Task Force and AI Steering Committee

Cross-campus bodies that guide responsible and ethical AI adoption and issue recommendations.

AI Accelerator in Teaching and Learning ↗

A cross-divisional leadership team, launched in 2026, that turns task force recommendations into action. Its work is described as "accelerating, enabling and stewarding." Members come from the CTL, the faculty, the library, and legal affairs.

Human-Centered AI concentration and AI Fundamentals minor ↗

Academic programs that bring AI literacy into the humanities and social sciences (see the Postscript).

Safe spaces to fail

The most important feature of any accelerator is not funding. It is permission to fail. Faculty are often evaluated on polished outcomes, which discourages risky experiments. A good accelerator protects time, offers support, and celebrates honest reports of what did not work, because those reports teach colleagues the most.

From events to culture

A single summit can inspire people. Culture forms when gatherings repeat, connect to ongoing programs, and produce visible results. The cycle looks like this:

From events to culture the cycle turns on its own; tap a step
  1. Gather people across boundaries.
  2. Experiment in real courses with support.
  3. Share results, including failures, publicly.
  4. Recognize contributors.
  5. Institutionalize what works in policy, curriculum, and budgets.
  6. Gather again, with a larger and more experienced community.
