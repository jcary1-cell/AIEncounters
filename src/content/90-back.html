The Curricular Blueprint

Practical Appendix: The UNC Charlotte Program Blueprint

UNC Charlotte's College of Humanities & Earth and Social Sciences (CHESS) offers a B.A. in Interdisciplinary Studies with a concentration in Human-Centered AI, along with a Minor in Artificial Intelligence Fundamentals open to all students. The concentration is paired with 18 credit hours in a disciplinary field, so every graduate combines AI literacy with depth in a traditional discipline.

Only one (either HCAI 3111 or HCAI 3112 is required for the Major concentration plus associated Disciplinary Depth Course work).

Minor in Artificial Intelligence Fundamentals (15 credit hours)

  • Required core (6 hours): HCAI 1100 and HCAI 2101
  • Electives (9 hours): chosen from more than 30 AI-related courses across the university, including philosophy (Philosophy of AI, Data Ethics, Philosophy of Technology), computing (Human-Centered Artificial Intelligence, Social Technology Design), engineering, business, finance, marketing, psychology, and political science

Program learning outcomes

Graduates of the concentration should be able to:

  1. Critically engage with and apply AI to analyze complex issues.
  2. Think and analyze in transferable ways, drawing on the humanities, earth, and social sciences.
  3. Demonstrate responsible AI literacy and evaluate the ethics of algorithmic systems.
  4. Combine AI and humanistic methods in interdisciplinary problem solving.

Transferable Implementation Guide

You do not need a new degree program to use this book. The twelve tools adapt to many settings.

SettingSuggested toolsHow to use them
General education coursesCognitive Friction Canvas, Dialectical Sparring Protocol, Sovereign Voice IndexEmbed one tool in a major assignment in any discipline. Ask students to submit the completed tool with their work.
First-year seminarsMeaningful Work Matrix, Syllabus as HandshakeUse in the first two weeks to build reflection habits and classroom community.
Capstone projectsLabor-to-Craft Audit, Algorithmic Provenance Audit, Architectural Co-Design CanvasRequire an audit of every AI tool used in the project, presented as part of the final defense.
Career development centersTie-Brokerage Map, Value Advocacy Playbook, Sovereign Voice IndexRun as workshops; integrate into advising and mock interviews.
Faculty developmentCo-Design Canvas, Syllabus as Handshake, Accelerator SandboxUse as the backbone of an AI teaching cohort.
Administrative leadershipAgile Task Force Canvas, Accelerator SandboxUse to design governance and gatherings across campus.

Five principles for adaptation

  1. Start small. One tool in one course is enough to begin.
  2. Keep the human at the center. Every tool should increase human judgment, voice, or connection.
  3. Make it visible. Ask learners to submit tools alongside their work, so the thinking is seen and valued.
  4. Collect stories. Document what happens. Stories persuade colleagues more than data alone. start here →
  5. Revisit annually. AI will change. Your use of these tools should too.

Coda

The Enduring Threadline

We began with encounters: a copywriter at a coffee machine, a student at midnight, a provost with an unsent email. Each faced the same question in a different form. When a machine can do this, what is left for me?

This book has tried to answer that question honestly. The answer is not that humans are safe from change. They are not. Work will shift, skills will be revalued, and institutions will be remade. Some of that change will be painful, and pretending otherwise helps no one.

But look again at what saved each person in these chapters. Marguerite's judgment. Sam's decision to choose struggle. Aisha's refusal of the easy answer. The three students who saw together what none could see alone. Rosa's grandmother and the stories only Rosa could tell. A department that chose conversation over surveillance. Eli's aunt's neighbor. Nadia's courage. A provost who listened. Three professors at a round table.

  • Marguerite's judgment

None of these was a better prompt. Every one was a human connection.

However sophisticated generative systems become, they remain mirrors of human pasts, built from what people have already written, said, and made. A mirror can be dazzling. It can show us things about ourselves we had not noticed. But it cannot choose what we do next.

That choice belongs to a thread older than any technology: our capacity for empathy, for being vulnerable with one another, for moral courage, and for shared struggle. That thread runs through every good classroom, every meaningful job, and every institution worth belonging to. It is what gives our thinking, learning, and work their lasting meaning.

Your next AI encounter is coming soon. Probably today. When it arrives, ask the question this book has asked in twelve different ways:

Does this bring me closer to the people and the work I care about, or does it carry me away from them?

Then choose. And choose together.
